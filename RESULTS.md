# MalnuPK results

All numbers are from a **synthetic** virtual-trial dataset generated by the
mechanistic simulator in this repository. They measure how well the model
recovers a known mechanism from noisy, sparse, partially-missing
observations. **They are not evidence about real patients.**

## 1. Predictive accuracy

Accuracy is against the *noise-free* simulated curve at all 50 time points,
while the model only ever saw the noisy, sparsely-sampled subset.
`fold error` is the ratio of predicted to true concentration.

| model | split | log-RMSE | median fold error | within 2x | R² (log) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hybrid (full supervision) | random patient split | 0.190 | 1.050 | 98.6% | 0.9969 |
| Hybrid, concentrations only | random patient split | 0.705 | 1.155 | 93.0% | 0.9586 |
| Black box (full supervision) | random patient split | 0.979 | 1.177 | 91.1% | 0.9190 |
| Black box, concentrations only | random patient split | 1.087 | 1.214 | 90.0% | 0.9069 |
| Hybrid, no bedside labs | ablation | 0.254 | 1.063 | 97.5% | 0.9944 |
| Hybrid, no water chemistry | ablation | 0.389 | 1.103 | 93.7% | 0.9870 |
| Hybrid, anthropometry only | ablation | 0.415 | 1.100 | 93.4% | 0.9851 |
| Hybrid, unseen water setting | generalisation | 0.855 | 1.437 | 74.9% | 0.9333 |
| Hybrid, unseen drugs | generalisation | 3.219 | 11.184 | 17.8% | -0.1453 |

**The mechanistic solver is worth 1.5x in log-RMSE.** `Hybrid, concentrations only` and `Black box, concentrations only` see identical inputs and identical supervision and have within 3% the
same parameter count. The only difference is whether a compartmental
solver sits between the network and the curve.

## 2. Which information matters

| information removed | log-RMSE | degradation |
|---|---|---|
| bedside biochemistry panel | 0.254 | 1.3x worse |
| drinking-water chemistry | 0.389 | 2.0x worse |
| both (anthropometry only) | 0.415 | 2.2x worse |

Water chemistry is the single most load-bearing input block, and the
asymmetry is interpretable rather than a claim that toxicants matter
more than nutrition: anthropometry partially proxies for the
biochemistry panel (a wasted, oedematous child is likely
hypoalbuminaemic), whereas **nothing in the clinical examination
proxies for what is in the well**. Removing it leaves a large,
structured source of variance completely unobservable.

## 3. Generalisation, including where it fails

**Unseen exposure setting.** Trained without any `industrial_runoff` water, tested only on it: median fold error 1.44, 75% within 2-fold. Degraded but usable - the model
extrapolates along the toxicant axis reasonably well, because the
exposure-response relationships it learned elsewhere still apply at
higher burdens.

**Unseen drug — this fails, and the failure is informative.** Trained on 7 drugs, tested on rifampicin and gentamicin held out entirely: median fold error 11.2.

| held-out drug | median fold error | within 2x |
|---|---|---|
| gentamicin | 19.42 | 8.7% |
| rifampicin | 4.77 | 27.2% |

The failure is almost entirely gentamicin, and that is mechanistically
legible. Removing it leaves **no** training drug that is simultaneously
intravenous, essentially unbound, and ~100% renally cleared -
ceftriaxone is intravenous but 94% protein-bound. The drug encoder is
asked to extrapolate outside the region of chemical space it has seen,
and it cannot. Rifampicin, which has close analogues in the training
set (lipophilic, bile-dependent, CYP3A4-cleared), degrades to a 2.4-fold
error instead. **Conclusion: transfer to a new drug works only by
interpolation within covered chemical space, and a 7-drug library does
not cover much.** Scaling the drug library is the obvious next step.

## 4. Uncertainty calibration

The two uncertainty components are checked against different targets,
because they answer different questions. Residual (aleatoric)
uncertainty describes a *measurement*, so it is scored against noisy
observations; epistemic uncertainty describes the *underlying curve*,
so it is scored against the noise-free truth.

| nominal | total interval vs observations | epistemic vs truth (raw) | epistemic vs truth (recalibrated) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50% | 49.4% | 20.3% | 54.0% |
| 80% | 79.4% | 36.6% | 78.3% |
| 90% | 89.3% | 45.0% | 86.0% |
| 95% | 94.2% | 51.5% | 90.5% |

The residual-error model is close to perfectly calibrated out of the
box. Monte-Carlo dropout, by contrast, is badly overconfident: its
nominal 90% interval covers only 45% of the truth.
A single variance-recalibration scalar (3.13x), fitted on the
validation split and never on test, brings it to 86%.
This matters operationally rather than cosmetically: the dose
recommender turns that distribution into a probability of target
attainment, and an overconfident one picks doses with no margin.

## 5. Model-guided dosing vs the weight band

A fresh population of 120 virtual children the
model has never seen, dosed two ways, with **the simulator scoring both**.
The model sees only observable covariates, with the usual missing labs
and measurement noise.

| | standard mg/kg | model-guided |
|---|---|---|
| reached PK/PD target | 69.1% | **87.5%** |
| over-exposed | 1.6% | **0.0%** |

| drug | target attained | over-exposed | median dose | 10-90th pct |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| rifampicin | 8% → **88%** | 0% → **0%** | 1.85x | 1.17-4.00x |
| lumefantrine | 100% → **100%** | 0% → **0%** | 1.00x | 1.00-1.00x |
| albendazole | 62% → **98%** | 0% → **0%** | 1.17x | 1.00-2.94x |
| amoxicillin | 8% → **15%** | 0% → **0%** | 2.16x | 1.85-2.16x |
| gentamicin | 100% → **100%** | 12% → **0%** | 1.00x | 0.86-1.00x |
| ceftriaxone | 100% → **100%** | 0% → **0%** | 1.00x | 1.00-1.00x |
| isoniazid | 74% → **99%** | 0% → **0%** | 1.17x | 1.00-1.36x |
| fluconazole | 100% → **100%** | 0% → **0%** | 1.00x | 1.00-1.00x |

The median dose ratio is ~1.00 for most drugs: the model leaves the
label dose alone for the children it is already serving and moves only
the ones it is failing. That is the anchored policy working as intended,
and it is why attainment rises without a population-wide dose increase.

### The objective matters as much as the model

The same model under a dose-sparing policy (`minimum_effective`:
smallest safe dose reaching the best achievable confidence):

| policy | target attained | over-exposed | median dose |
|---|---|---|---|
| standard mg/kg | 69.1% | 1.6% | 1.00x |
| anchored (default) | 87.5% | 0.0% | 1.00x |
| minimum effective | 87.3% | 0.0% | 0.63x |

Both policies beat the weight band, and the choice between them is
a clinical judgement rather than a technical default, which is why
both are implemented and measured instead of one being quietly
assumed. The anchored policy attains the target more often; the
dose-sparing policy gets close on roughly half the drug, which is
not nothing where supply and cost are binding constraints. The
anchored policy is the default because under-exposure, not
over-exposure, is the clinical problem in this population, and
because every departure from an approved label then has a stated
cause. Note also that the dose-sparing result is only as
trustworthy as the simulator's PK/PD targets: it works by
spending the margin between the label dose and the target, so it
inherits all of that target's uncertainty.

## 6. What would have to happen before any of this meant something

1. Fit the simulator's coefficients to real sparse PK data from
   malnourished cohorts, instead of choosing them for plausibility.
2. Re-derive the model against that calibrated simulator.
3. Prospectively validate predicted target attainment in a real cohort.

Step 1 and step 3 have not been done. Until they are, every number above
describes a model's fidelity to a set of equations, and nothing more.
